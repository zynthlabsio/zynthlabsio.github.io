How to brief a research task so you get exactly what you need

Research · 17 November 2026

A research report can be accurate, well written — and still useless. That almost never happens because the researcher was lazy. It happens because the brief left the real question unspoken, so the researcher answered a different one.

1. What decision will this inform?

Start with the decision, not the topic. “Research the market for X” is a topic. “Should we launch in city A or city B first?” is a decision. The second version tells the researcher what evidence actually matters, and what can safely be left out.

2. What is in scope — and what is out?

Name the geography, the time period, the type of company or product, and anything you have already ruled out. Two lines of scope in the brief save pages of irrelevant findings later.

3. Which sources count?

Say whether you want only primary sources (official sites, filings, first-party data), or whether reputable secondary coverage is acceptable. If some sources are off-limits or must be included, list them.

4. What should the delivery look like?

A one-page summary with a recommendation, a detailed brief with sources, or a spreadsheet of findings are three different products. Pick the format, the length, and whether you want sources linked for every claim — for most business decisions, you do.

5. When do you need it, and what does “done” mean?

Give a real deadline and one sentence on what a finished delivery lets you do. “Done means I can choose a supplier from the comparison table without further research” is a testable finish line.

A brief you can copy

  1. Decision: …
  2. Scope in / out: …
  3. Sources that count: …
  4. Format and length: …
  5. Deadline and definition of done: …

Five short answers. That is the whole brief — and it is the difference between a report you file away and a report you act on.
